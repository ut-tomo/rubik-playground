About

This playground lets you manipulate a Rubik's Cube intuitively using Singmaster notation:

Singmaster notation:

$U$: turn the up face 90° clockwise (Up)
$D$: turn the down face 90° clockwise (Down)
$L$: turn the left face 90° clockwise (Left)
$R$: turn the right face 90° clockwise (Right)
$F$: turn the front face 90° clockwise (Front)
$B$: turn the back face 90° clockwise (Back)

The face colors are fixed: up is white, down is yellow, left is orange, right is red, front is green, and back is blue. “Clockwise” is always from the perspective of looking straight at the face being turned.

Corner and edge pieces are named in the same way, with u, d, l, r, f, and b corresponding to their respective faces. For example, ufr is the corner where the up, front, and right faces meet—here, the white-red-green piece.

An apostrophe (') after a symbol means applying the inverse move, while a 2 means performing the move twice in a row—in other words, making a $180°$ turn.

The buttons under Quick Moves apply these basic moves. Under Algorithm, you can string them together into a sequence. When typing a sequence, be sure to put a regular space between each move.

At the end of the page, there’s a short group-theoretic explanation, too. The idea is to use the Rubik’s Cube to help build some intuition and to make its moves easier to understand in group-theoretic terms.

For reference, here are a few well-known Rubik’s Cube move sequences and the corresponding Singmaster notation. Copy & paste them in and give them a try.

• Sexy move R U R' U'

• Right trigger (right turn) U R U' R'

• Rubik's maneuver L R' F' L R' D' L R' B2 L' R D' L' R F' L' R U2

Try repeating each of the first two six times and check that the cube returns to where it started! Sequences meant to make partial swaps like these fall into the category of commutators (A → B → A' → B'). I’m calling the third one the “Rubik’s maneuver” here. That name (probably) comes from the Tsukuda method, and it seems to be used for all sorts of different sequences. It flips a pair of opposite edges on the same face. I think it’s more common to write it using “$M$,” which turns the middle slice, but it can (obviously) be written without $M$ as well. It’s quite a special move (group-theoretically?).

Conjugation: a well-known lemma on permutations

Seeing conjugation on the cube

It seems that people who are good at solving the Rubik’s Cube use the following lemma implicitly, over and over.

Here we compose permutations from left to right, in the order the moves are performed, to match cube notation.

Lemma. Let $r \in S_n$ be a permutation, and let $i$ and $j$ be distinct elements of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$. Suppose $s$ is a permutation that sends $i$ to $j$; that is, \[ s(i) = j. \] Then the permutation $s^r = r^{-1}sr$ sends $r(i)$ to $r(j)$; in other words, \[ s^r(r(i)) = r(j). \]

In particular, let $i_1, i_2, \ldots, i_k$ be distinct elements of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, and let $s$ be the cycle that sends $i_j$ to $i_{j+1}$. Then $s^r = r^{-1}sr$ is the cycle that sends $r(i_j)$ to $r(i_{j+1})$.

Elements $a, b \in G$ related by $b = g^{-1}ag$ are called conjugate, and the Rubik’s Cube gives you a particularly good feel for conjugation.

A striking example is R' D2 R B' U2 B repeated twice: it twists ufr clockwise and dbl counterclockwise (you can try it in the playground above).

At first glance, this looks complicated, and it seems hard to keep track of everything at once. But try applying R' D2 R and B' U2 B separately, twice each (for example, R' D2 R R' D2 R), and you’ll notice that the cube returns to its starting state. Then watch how the edge pieces move while applying R' D2 R B' U2 B: you’ll see that R' D2 R and B' U2 B act independently of each other. In other words, once you realize that only the corners need to be tracked under R' D2 R B' U2 B, the result is fairly intuitive even before getting into the lemma and all that—but let’s give it a little more explanation here.

What the lemma says is this: if $s$ causes $i \rightarrow j$, then under $r^{-1}sr$, the action of $s$ is copied to wherever $r$ moves things. Taking R' D2 R as an example, the permutation produced by D2 takes place where R' has moved the pieces (see the figure below). So, looking only at the corners, the permutation produced by D2 \[ D2 = \begin{pmatrix} e & f & g & h \\ g & h & e & f \end{pmatrix} \] takes place where R' has moved the pieces, so \[ (D2)^{R'} = \begin{pmatrix} R'(e) & R'(f) & R'(g) & R'(h) \\ R'(g) & R'(h) & R'(e) & R'(f) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e & b & f & h \\ f & h & e & b \end{pmatrix}. \] Try the moves on the cube, and you should be able to see this for yourself. With this way of thinking, the pieces’ positions and orientations become easy to track, and you’ll see that the whole sequence twists two corners.
